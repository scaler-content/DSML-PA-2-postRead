Ground floor

Metrics & KPIs

Start at the base: what exactly is a "metric," what makes one a "KPI," and why do interviewers care so much?

Product metric

A quantifiable data point a business tracks and analyses to evaluate the success of its product.

  • →Used to optimise product strategy for growth
  • →The right metrics lead to smarter decisions across development
KPI (Key Performance Indicator)

A metric important enough that the company holds itself accountable to it — quantifiable evidence of what's resonating with customers and what isn't.

  • →Can relate to user needs, size, quality, growth, or comfort
  • →The metric a leader puts on a slide and a team's bonus depends on
Every metric is a data point — but not every data point earns "KPI" status

"Total button clicks" is a metric. "Weekly Active Users" is a KPI that Netflix, Spotify and Uber would restructure teams around. A KPI is simply the metric important enough to be held accountable to.

Classifying metrics

Vanity vs. Actionable

There's no fixed taxonomy of metrics — but the most useful first cut is vanity vs. actionable, based on whether a number actually guides a decision.

AspectVanity metricsActionable metrics
NatureSuperficial — showcase positive but shallow numbersTied to the core goals of the business or product
EmphasisQuantity over quality; little real insightQuality over quantity; focus on critical data
ActionabilityNo direct link to actions that improve performanceClosely tied to specific improving actions
Purpose"Ego metrics" — boost an ego with impressive numbersGoal-oriented; track progress toward outcomes
ExamplesTotal visitors, followers, page views, downloadsConversion rate, CAC, CLV, retention, revenue per user
📊 A social-media influencer may have 5M followers but drive a 0.001% CTR when promoting a product. Big number, no decision — that's vanity in one line.
Quiz 1 · Vanity vs Actionable
A growth team celebrates crossing 10 million cumulative app downloads. As the data analyst, which metric would you push them to track instead, because it is actionable rather than vanity?
Answer: C. Downloads, followers and cumulative page views only ever go up and look impressive, but they don't point to a decision — they're vanity. Week-1 retention is tied to a specific action: if it's low, you fix onboarding or stop over-spending on acquisition. Actionable metrics connect to decisions and can move down as well as up.
The metric hierarchy

One Star, Two Tiers Beneath It

Companies grow by stacking metrics into three levels: a single guiding North Star, a few Primary (L1) metrics that feed it, and granular Supporting (L2) metrics underneath.

⭐ North Star / Focus Metric

The single most important measure of success for a company. It should be:

  • →A direct reflection of the company's mission
  • →An indicator of the value the company brings to customers
  • →The only one of its kind — multiple NSMs create confusion
  • →The answer to: "the one metric that best represents our desired outcome"

The test: which metric, if it increased today, would most accelerate the business's flywheel?

Real-world example

Airbnb doesn't obsess over sign-ups; it tracks Booked Nights — because a booked night means a guest and a host both won. The test: does your one number go up only when your customer actually succeeds?

Quiz 2 · North Star Metric
A meditation app's mission is to help users build a daily mindfulness habit. Which of these is the strongest North Star Metric?
Answer: C. A North Star reflects the mission and the value actually delivered, and is the only one of its kind. "Daily active users who complete a session" captures users genuinely building the habit the product promises. Installs and reviews are vanity; library size is a supply/input metric, not delivered value.

🎯 Level-1 / Primary Metric

  • →Depicts the desired outcome of a particular product, team or initiative — unlike the NSM, which is the whole company.
  • →Should directly contribute to the focus metric, or act as a check that the product is growing in a healthy direction.
  • →Can be more valuable than the NSM in the short term: narrower focus, tighter feedback loop, closer to the specific team.
Worked example

If the focus metric is Weekly Active Users, a good L1 metric is 7-day retention — so you aren't burning marketing spend acquiring users who leave after a day or two.

🔬 Level-2 / Supporting Metric

  • →The most granular indicators, used to track progress at the lowest level.
  • →A signal that the primary metric is moving in the right direction.
  • →Often a primary metric sliced by platform, region or segment.
Worked example

Take the retention example one step further: an L2 metric could be iOS app retention, or the retention of a specific region or customer segment.

Quiz 3 · L1 vs L2
A product's focus metric is Weekly Active Users, and its Level-1 metric is 7-day retention. Which is the best example of a Level-2 (supporting) metric?
Answer: B. Level-2 metrics are the most granular — usually a Level-1 metric sliced by platform, region or segment. "7-day retention for iOS users" is exactly the primary metric broken down by platform. Company-wide revenue is broader, not more granular, so it can't be an L2.

Interview lens

Two Types of Metric Question

Companies like Facebook and Google use metric questions to test critical thinking and communication. The first thing they score is whether you recognise which type you're facing.

Type 1 · Metric definition

Can you define metrics that show the health of a product or feature?

"What metrics would you use to determine success for Facebook Sponsored Posts?"

Many metrics could apply (impressions, clicks, return on ad spend…). The interviewer wants to hear you select the most important ones through a rigorous process → today's 5-step framework.

Type 2 · Metric change

Do you know what to do when a key metric moves up or down for no apparent reason?

"Why did the Facebook event clicks jump 15%?"

This is essentially root cause analysis — you already met it last class. CRIED + TROPiCS is your ready-made answer.

The callback

Metric-change questions are the "why did it move?" problems from Lecture 1 — so CRIED and TROPiCS handle them. Today we build a framework for the other type: metric definition.

The definition framework

Describe → Goal → Journey → Map → Evaluate

A repeatable five-step process for any metric-definition question. Step through it.

1
Step 1 of 5

Describe the feature

Explain your understanding of the feature — what problem it solves and how. Make sure everyone is on the same page about the problem before you measure anything.

Why it matters

Skipping this is the classic trap — you end up measuring a feature you and the interviewer pictured differently.

2
Step 2 of 5

Determine the goal

You're measuring the success of a feature in achieving a specific goal. Get clarity on exactly what that goal is before choosing any metric.

Anchor everything here

Every metric you propose later should trace back to this goal — that's how you avoid a pile of vanity numbers.

3
Step 3 of 5

Walk the customer journey

Understand the funnel — walk the user from the start to the end of their interaction with the feature. This surfaces potential behaviours that can help or hurt the feature's success.

The habit

Whenever you must define metrics, walk the user down the funnel and measure each step — the same lens behind AAAERR from last class.

4
Step 4 of 5

Map & quantify user behaviours

Map the behaviours along the journey that push the goal up or down, and quantify them. The customer-journey phases give you the checklist — tap each to see what you'd measure.

1
Awareness
Do users know it exists?
▼

Reach and discovery — % of users who have seen or found the feature.

2
Acquisition
Are new users coming in?
▼

New users or customers entering — sign-ups, first-time adopters.

3
Activation
Do they reach first value?
▼

Adoption — % who take the first key action that delivers value.

4
Engagement
Are they interacting?
▼

Frequency and depth of use over time.

5
Retention
Do they come back?
▼

Return behaviour — D1/D7/D30 retention, churn.

6
Monetization
Is it earning?
▼

Revenue signals — revenue per user, conversion to paid.

7
Referral
Do they bring others?
▼

Virality — invites sent, referral conversion.

5
Step 5 of 5

Evaluate your metrics

You now have a list of candidate metrics. Evaluate them against meaningful criteria and keep the ones that matter most.

Quiz 4 · Capstone — pick the type & the approach
An interviewer says: "YouTube's watch time dropped 8% last week with no obvious cause — how would you investigate?" What type of question is this, and what's the correct approach?
Answer: B. A metric moving "for no apparent reason" is a metric-change question — essentially RCA. Rule out instrumentation/logging first, then internal factors (time, region, platform, segments, cannibalization) and external ones (competitors, seasonality) — the CRIED/TROPiCS approach. The 5-step framework is for the other type ("what metrics would you use for X?"), so recognising the type first is exactly what interviewers score.
Interview corner

Practice Questions

Frame your own answer first — then reveal the approach.

Q1 — What metrics would you use to measure success for Facebook Sponsored Posts?
Metric definition
Describe: A paid post an advertiser shows to a target audience in-feed.
Goal: Drive advertiser value (clicks/conversions) without wrecking user experience.
Journey → Map: Awareness (impressions, reach) → Engagement (CTR, likes) → Monetization (ROAS, CPC) → guardrail (hide/report rate).
Evaluate: Rank by reach/impact/confidence/effort; lead with ROAS and CTR as the headline metrics.
Q2 — What's a strong North Star Metric for a food-delivery app, and why not "app downloads"?
North Star
Candidate: Orders successfully delivered per week — up only when a customer actually gets value.
Why not downloads: vanity — it only goes up and doesn't reflect delivered value (recall the 2M-downloads trap).
Test: would it accelerate the flywheel if it rose today? Delivered orders → yes; downloads → not necessarily.
Q3 — Signups are up 12% but revenue is flat. How would you investigate?
Metric change
Recognise the type: a metric-change question → RCA, not the 5-step framework.
Rule out: tracking/attribution bug inflating signups?
TROPiCS: Segmentation (are the new signups low-intent?), Region/Platform (one channel spiking?), Cannibalization.
Diagnose: likely low-quality acquisition — signups aren't activating/monetizing. Watch L1 activation, not the vanity signup count.
Q4 — Build a 3-level metric hierarchy for a video-streaming app.
Hierarchy
North Star: total watch time (value delivered — people actually watching).
Primary (L1): 7-day retention; average sessions per active user.
Supporting (L2): iOS 7-day retention; watch time by region; completion rate per content category.
Check: every L1 feeds the North Star; every L2 is an L1 sliced by platform/region/segment — none are vanity.

Key Takeaways

1
A KPI is a metric you're held accountable to

Every metric is a data point; a KPI is the one important enough to put on a slide and tie a bonus to.

2
Vanity looks impressive; actionable drives a decision

If a number only ever goes up and points to no action, it's vanity. Actionable metrics can fall — and tell you what to fix.

3
Stack metrics into three levels

One North Star (whole company) → Primary L1 (team/product) → Supporting L2 (a primary sliced by platform/region/segment).

4
Name the question type before you answer

Definition ("what metrics for X?") → 5-step framework. Change ("why did it move?") → CRIED/TROPiCS. Interviewers score this first.

5
Define metrics with Describe → Goal → Journey → Map → Evaluate

Walk the user down the funnel, quantify each behaviour, then rank on reach, impact, confidence and effort.
